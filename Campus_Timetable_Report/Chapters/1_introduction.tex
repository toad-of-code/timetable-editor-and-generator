Every semester, universities face the same daunting puzzle: how do you fit hundreds of courses, dozens of professors, and a limited set of classrooms into a semester wide schedule that actually works? This is the University Timetabling Problem (UTP), and it is famously NP-hard---meaning there is no known algorithm that can guarantee a perfect solution in reasonable time. In this chapter, we introduce the IIITA Timetabling System, explain why we built it, and lay out the structure of the rest of this thesis.

%-----------------------------------
\section{Background and Motivation}

Academic timetabling boils down to assigning events---lectures, tutorials, and lab sessions---to time slots, rooms, and professors, all while respecting a web of constraints. Some of these constraints are absolute: you cannot double-book a professor or schedule two classes in the same room at the same time. Others are softer preferences: students would rather not have large gaps between classes, and professors would prefer to teach in a single half of the day rather than being spread across morning and afternoon.

At IIIT Allahabad, like many Indian universities, scheduling has traditionally been a manual affair. Academic coordinators draft timetables in Microsoft Excel, painstakingly checking for conflicts by eye. Excel is flexible, but it has no understanding of what a ``professor'' or a ``room'' actually means---it just sees text in cells. As a result, catching mistakes like a professor being assigned to two rooms at once depends entirely on human vigilance. With hundreds of courses and thousands of students, errors are inevitable.

The problems do not end once a timetable is published. Distributing a static PDF or spreadsheet means that any change---a room swap, a new faculty assignment---requires sending out the entire document again. Students end up working from outdated copies, and nobody can easily answer simple questions like ``which rooms are free at 2 PM on Wednesday?''

These frustrations motivated us to build the IIITA Timetabling System. We wanted a single, centralised platform that could serve as the definitive source of truth for all scheduling data---one that could automatically generate conflict-free timetables, let administrators fine-tune them interactively, and give every stakeholder (students, faculty, administrators) a tailored, always-up-to-date view of their schedule.

\section{Problem Statement}

The core problem we set out to solve is straightforward: traditional spreadsheet-based timetable management at IIITA is slow, error-prone, and fundamentally static. More specifically, the existing process suffers from:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unstructured Data:} Timetables live in Excel files full of merged cells, inconsistent naming, and implicit relationships that are nearly impossible for software to parse reliably.
    \item \textbf{Manual Conflict Detection:} Spotting clashes---room double-bookings, professor overlaps, section conflicts---is done by hand, and mistakes regularly slip through.
    \item \textbf{No Automated Generation:} There is no institutional tool to compute a conflict-free timetable from scratch. The entire process is manual and iterative, often taking weeks of back-and-forth.
    \item \textbf{One-Size-Fits-All Views:} A single master timetable is overwhelming. Students must hunt through it to find their classes, and professors have no quick way to see just their teaching schedule.
    \item \textbf{No Resource Visibility:} Administrators have no easy way to check which rooms are free at a given time or how well campus space is being utilised.
    \item \textbf{Static Distribution:} Once a timetable is published as a PDF, updating it means redistributing the entire file---and hoping everyone sees the new version.
\end{itemize}

Our goal, then, was to build a system that bridges the gap between messy spreadsheets and structured data, automates schedule generation, provides interactive editing tools, and delivers dynamic, role-specific views of the timetable.

\section{Objectives of the Study}

We set ourselves seven concrete objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Heuristic Data Ingestion:} Build a parsing engine that can read IIITA's complex Excel timetable files, extract metadata (subjects, faculty, rooms), resolve merged cells, and normalise everything into a clean relational schema.
    \item \textbf{Real-Time Clash Detection:} Develop algorithms that instantly flag scheduling conflicts across rooms, professors, and student sections the moment data is loaded.
    \item \textbf{Role-Based Views:} Create tailored interfaces for each stakeholder:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item A filtered student schedule with section and elective selection.
        \item A dedicated Professor View showing only their teaching commitments.
        \item A Master Occupancy View and Free Room Finder for campus administrators.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Export and Reporting:} Integrate PDF generation for individual schedules and Google Sheets export for administrators who need professionally formatted, shareable documents.
    \item \textbf{Automated Timetable Generation:} Implement a (1+1) Evolution Strategy solver that evaluates candidates against ten hard constraints and three soft constraints, adapts its step size via the 1/5th success rule, supports warm-starting from existing schedules, and respects cross-semester constraints through locked sessions.
    \item \textbf{Interactive Timetable Editor:} Build a drag-and-drop interface for refining timetables with real-time conflict highlighting, inline room and professor editing, and a one-click LNS auto-fix for resolving remaining clashes.
    \item \textbf{Generator Wizard:} Create a guided, multi-step workflow that walks administrators through cluster selection, home room mapping, professor assignment, and solver configuration.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and Limitations}

This project covers the full design, development, and deployment of the IIITA Timetabling System: a React and TypeScript frontend, a Supabase (PostgreSQL) backend, and a (1+1)-ES solver running entirely in the browser. While the system is tailored to IIIT Allahabad's specific timetable format, the architecture is general enough to adapt to other institutions.

That said, there are a few important limitations to keep in mind:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Parser Sensitivity:} Our Excel parser relies on heuristic rules tuned to IIITA's template. Major deviations from that format may require tweaking the parsing logic.
    \item \textbf{Single-Threaded Solver:} The solver runs on the browser's main thread using batched \texttt{setTimeout} calls. This works well for current problem sizes but could become a bottleneck for much larger institutions.
    \item \textbf{Heuristic Optimality:} Evolution Strategies are meta-heuristics---they reliably find feasible schedules (zero hard violations), but the solutions are not guaranteed to be globally optimal for soft constraints. Quality depends on the number of generations and the search landscape.
\end{itemize}

\section{Organization of the Thesis}

The rest of this thesis is organised as follows:

\textbf{Chapter 2: Literature Review} surveys existing work on university timetabling, covering the theoretical foundations of UTP as an NP-hard problem and reviewing algorithmic approaches from the literature.

\textbf{Chapter 3: Technologies and Frameworks} explains our technology choices---React 19, TypeScript, Tailwind CSS, Vite, and Supabase---and why each was selected.

\textbf{Chapter 4: System Architecture and Design} walks through the database schema, the data ingestion pipeline, the solver module structure, and the client-side component routing.

\textbf{Chapter 5: Implementation and Results} is the heart of the thesis. It covers the Excel parser, the clash detection algorithms, the (1+1)-ES solver and its constraint system, the drag-and-drop editor with LNS auto-fix, and the Generator Wizard.

\textbf{Chapter 6: Conclusion and Future Work} sums up what we achieved, reflects on the challenges we faced, and outlines future directions such as parallelised solvers and hybrid meta-heuristics.
